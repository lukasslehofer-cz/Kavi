How long to rest coffee after roasting before it tastes its best? Many people believe the best coffee is the one roasted yesterday. In fact, freshly roasted coffee needs some time for carbon dioxide to escape and for the flavors to settle. Without it, coffee tends to taste sharp and unbalanced, and it is hard to brew well. This process is called degassing. In this article we explain what happens to coffee after roasting, how many days to rest it for filter and for espresso, and how to tell when a coffee is at its peak.

Quick answer: Rest filter coffee for about 3–7 days after roasting and espresso coffee for 7–14 days. Very light espresso roasts may need 2–4 weeks. After that, specialty coffee tastes best up to roughly 4–6 weeks from roasting, then gradually loses its aroma.

What happens to coffee after roasting

Hundreds of chemical reactions take place inside the bean during roasting. Sugars caramelize and aromatic compounds form, and as a by-product so does a large amount of carbon dioxide (CO₂). The gas stays trapped in the bean's cells and is gradually released after roasting: fastest in the first few days, then more and more slowly.

That is why bags of specialty coffee have a one-way valve: gas can escape from the bag, but air can't get in. Without the valve, fresh coffee would inflate the bag or even burst it. If you gently squeeze a bag of fresh coffee, you can smell it through the valve. That is the escaping CO₂ carrying aromatic compounds with it.

Why freshly roasted coffee doesn't taste its best

Carbon dioxide in the bean gets in the way of brewing. When you pour water over coffee that is too fresh, the gas escapes violently and stops the water from reaching all the coffee particles evenly. The result is uneven extraction: some of the coffee is under-extracted, some over-extracted.

Here is how you notice it in the cup:

  • With filter coffee: the coffee foams up wildly when you pour, forming a large bubbly dome, the water flows through unevenly and the taste is flat, bitter or muted.
  • With espresso: the shot runs unevenly, the crema is excessively thick, pale and bubbly and quickly falls apart. The taste is often sharply sour, salty or harsh, and every shot tastes different.

On top of that, the flavors themselves are still settling after roasting. In the first few days coffee often tastes green and unbalanced; sweetness and complexity only develop later.

How many days to rest coffee

The exact time depends on the coffee and the brewing method. Take these numbers as a starting point:

Filter coffee: 3–7 days

Filter coffee is brewed without pressure and with longer contact between coffee and water, so a little leftover CO₂ doesn't matter as much. Most filter coffees are in good shape about three to seven days after roasting. Very light roasts of dense, high-altitude beans may need longer: ten days or even two weeks.

Before brewing, don't forget the bloom: first wet the coffee with a small amount of water (about twice the weight of the coffee) and let it bubble for 30–45 seconds. The gas is released and the rest of the water then flows through evenly.

Espresso coffee: 7–14 days

Espresso is more sensitive to freshness. Water is forced through the coffee at around nine bars of pressure in a few dozen seconds, so every bubble of CO₂ disrupts extraction. Rest espresso coffees for at least a week, ideally ten to fourteen days. With very light espresso roasts, many roasters and baristas agree that the peak comes only after two, three or sometimes even four weeks.

Bean-to-cup machine: 10–14 days

Bean-to-cup machines make espresso, so a similar rule applies. Fresh coffee can also cause problems with even flow in these machines. If coffee from your machine tastes sharp or unbalanced, try waiting a few days.

Moka pot, French press and AeroPress: 4–10 days

These methods sit somewhere between filter and espresso. The French press and AeroPress are forgiving; with a moka pot it is worth waiting at least a week, because the coffee is brewed under mild pressure.

What the resting time depends on

  • Roast level. Dark-roasted beans are more porous, CO₂ escapes from them faster and they are ready sooner, but they also go stale faster. Light-roasted beans are denser and release gas more slowly, so they need a longer rest.
  • Bean density. Coffees grown at high altitudes have denser beans and degas more slowly.
  • Processing. Natural and fermented coffees may behave differently from washed ones, although the differences tend to be smaller than with roast level.
  • Temperature and packaging. Degassing is faster in warm conditions. An unopened bag with a valve slows down oxidation, so the coffee can mature undisturbed.

When coffee is at its best and when it is stale

You can picture the life of roasted coffee as a curve with four phases:

  1. Too fresh (0 to a few days): lots of CO₂, unbalanced flavor, difficult to brew.
  2. Peak (roughly 1–4 weeks): the flavors are developed, sweetness and acidity are in balance and the coffee is easy to brew.
  3. Plateau (roughly 4–8 weeks): the coffee is still excellent, it just gradually loses its most delicate aromas.
  4. Decline (after 2–3 months): the aromas fade, the flavor flattens and papery, stale notes appear.

The boundaries aren't sharp and vary from coffee to coffee. As a general rule, though, specialty coffee tastes best up to roughly 4–6 weeks from roasting, and once the bag is open it is ideally used within two to four weeks.

How to tell if coffee is too fresh or already stale

Coffee that is too fresh:

  • forms a large dome and foams up wildly when you pour water over it for filter,
  • makes espresso with an excessively thick, bubbly crema,
  • tastes sharp and unbalanced, and varies from cup to cup,
  • comes in a visibly puffed-up bag.

Stale coffee:

  • barely bubbles when you pour water over it,
  • makes espresso with a thin crema that disappears quickly,
  • tastes flat, lacks sweetness, and comes across as papery or woody,
  • smells only faintly when you open the bag.

One exception to keep in mind: dark-roasted coffees have an oily surface from the start. With light-roasted specialty coffee, however, an oily sheen on the beans is usually a sign that the coffee is stale.

Why to look for the roast date on the bag

The best-before date you find on supermarket coffee tells you little about freshness; it is usually set a year or two after production. For specialty coffee, the key information is the roast date. Only that tells you whether to open the coffee right away or give it a few more days. We cover what else to look for on the bag in How to Choose Coffee Beans.

A practical tip: if you get coffee roasted two days ago, leave it in its unopened bag for a few days and finish your previous bag in the meantime. If the coffee was roasted two to three weeks ago, it is ready to go.

How to work with resting time in practice

Theory is one thing, your morning routine another. A few simple habits will help you drink every coffee at its best:

  • Check the roast date as soon as you unpack. Put aside coffee roasted a few days ago and open the older one first.
  • Open filter coffee before espresso. If you have both from the same week, start with the filter. The espresso can mature in the meantime.
  • Don't keep too many bags open at once. Two open coffees is a sensible maximum, so none of them goes stale for no reason.
  • Note the days since roasting. When a coffee tastes exceptionally good, write down how many days after roasting it was. You will soon learn when the coffees you like reach their peak.
  • Don't be afraid to adjust your grinder. As coffee ages and releases less gas, the way it flows changes too. With espresso you will probably notice that after a few weeks it runs faster; just grind a little finer and the taste comes back into balance.
  • Split large stocks. If you have more coffee than you can drink in a month, freeze the rested bags in smaller portions and take them out one at a time.

One more piece of advice: don't treat any of these numbers as dogma. One coffee will be great on day five, another only opens up after three weeks. Taste, observe and let your palate guide you. You can learn more about tasting coffee mindfully in How to Taste Coffee at Home.

Resting is not storing

Resting after roasting and long-term storage are two different things. In the first few weeks the coffee matures; after that, you are just protecting it from going stale. Store coffee in its original bag or an airtight container, somewhere dry and dark, at room temperature. Don't put it in the fridge, where it absorbs odors and moisture. If you have more coffee than you can drink in time, you can freeze it in small portions. You will find a detailed guide in How to Store Coffee and Keep It Fresh.

Frequently asked questions

Can I drink coffee the day after it was roasted?

You can, and it won't harm you. It just probably won't taste as good as it will in a week: it will be sharper and less sweet, and harder to brew, especially as espresso.

What does the roast date on the bag mean?

The day the coffee was roasted. It tells you whether the coffee is ready to drink or needs a few more days. For specialty coffee, it is the most important information on the bag.

Why does a coffee bag have a valve?

So that the carbon dioxide released by fresh coffee can escape from the bag while no air gets in. Without a valve, the bag would puff up.

Is it worth freezing coffee?

Yes, if you can't drink it within about two months. Freeze it in small, well-sealed portions and let it come to room temperature before opening, so that moisture doesn't condense on the beans. Never refreeze coffee once it has been thawed.

Why does espresso made from fresh coffee taste sour?

Carbon dioxide prevents even extraction and the coffee is under-extracted, and under-extracted coffee tastes sour and salty. Try waiting a few days or grinding a little finer.
