Coffee varieties matter as much to what ends up in your cup as grape varieties do to wine. Riesling tastes different from Sauvignon Blanc, and in the same way Gesha tastes different from Bourbon or Kenya's SL28. Yet most people never even notice the variety on the bag. In this article we explain what a coffee variety is and why it matters, introduce the most important ones, from the classic Typica and Bourbon to today's stars, and tell you why a kilogram of Panamanian Gesha sold for more than 30,000 dollars.

Quick answer: A variety is a "breed" of coffee plant within a species. Most specialty coffee comes from the arabica species, which has hundreds of varieties. The best known include Typica and Bourbon (the ancestors of many others), Caturra and Catuaí (widespread in Central and South America), Kenya's SL28 and SL34, El Salvador's Pacamara, and Gesha, the most expensive and famous variety in the world, with floral notes of jasmine and bergamot.

Species or variety? What's the difference

Coffee is a plant genus with dozens of species. For the coffee we drink, two matter most: arabica (Coffea arabica) and robusta (Coffea canephora). Arabica is more delicate and aromatic and forms the basis of specialty coffee; robusta is hardier, more bitter and has more caffeine. We compare them in detail in Arabica vs. Robusta: What's the Difference?

A variety is a variant within a species, just as Golden Delicious and Jonagold are varieties of apple. Each arabica variety differs in appearance, yield, disease resistance and, above all, flavor potential.

Why the coffee variety matters

  • Flavor. Some varieties have naturally bright acidity and fruitiness, others sweetness and a full body. Gesha smells of jasmine, SL28 of blackcurrant, Bourbon of caramel and red fruit.
  • Yield. The best-tasting varieties are often the least productive, so farmers have to choose between quality and quantity.
  • Resistance. A major threat to coffee plants is coffee leaf rust, a fungal disease that can wipe out entire plantations. Modern varieties tend to resist it better; the old classics often don't.
  • Growing conditions. Some varieties only excel at high altitudes, while others also do well lower down.

Variety isn't everything, though. The final flavor also depends on where the coffee is grown, how it is processed and how it is roasted. The same variety can taste different in Colombia than in Ethiopia.

Typica and Bourbon: the two mothers of most arabicas

The history of coffee is a history of travel. Arabica comes from Ethiopia, from where it reached Yemen. In the 17th and 18th centuries, traders and colonists carried it around the world, and almost all coffee grown outside Africa descends from a handful of plants from that era. Two main lines came out of them:

Typica

The line the Dutch took via India to Java and later to botanical gardens in Europe, from where it reached the Caribbean and Latin America. Typica has tall, slender trees and a low yield, but excellent flavor: clean and sweet, with gentle acidity. It is fairly rare today because it is prone to disease.

Bourbon

Named after the island of Bourbon in the Indian Ocean (today's Réunion), where the French brought it from Yemen in the 18th century. Bourbon is sweet and complex, with notes of caramel and red fruit and a bright but pleasant acidity. It comes in several color variants (red, yellow and pink) depending on the color of the ripe cherries. It is typical of countries such as Rwanda, Burundi and El Salvador.

Gesha (Geisha): the world's most famous variety

No other variety has changed the world of specialty coffee as much as Gesha. Its story begins in Ethiopia, in the area around the Gori Gesha forest, where it was collected in the 1930s. Via research stations in East Africa and Costa Rica it eventually made its way to Panama. For decades it grew there unnoticed: farmers planted it mainly for its resistance, not its flavor.

The turning point came in 2004. The Peterson family of Hacienda La Esmeralda in Panama noticed that the Gesha from a high-altitude plot tasted completely different from every other coffee, and entered it in the national Best of Panama competition. The judges were stunned, and Gesha became the most sought-after coffee in the world almost overnight.

What Gesha tastes like

A good Gesha resembles a delicate tea more than a coffee. Typical are floral notes of jasmine and bergamot, fruit notes of peach, apricot and citrus, a light body and a long, sweet finish. It shows best as filter coffee; in espresso or with milk its delicacy would get lost.

Why Gesha is so expensive

Panamanian Gesha keeps breaking price records at auction. At the 2025 Best of Panama auction, a washed Gesha from Hacienda La Esmeralda sold for $30,204 per kilogram. The 20-kilogram lot fetched more than $600,000, and the coffee scored a record 98 points in the competition. A year later, in 2026, the top lot of the same auction, a Gesha from Lamastus Family Estates, sold for $18,004 per kilogram. Several factors lie behind these prices:

  • Low yield. Gesha produces far fewer cherries than modern varieties.
  • Demanding cultivation. It only develops its exceptional flavor at high altitudes and with careful attention.
  • Huge demand. Buyers from Asia and the Middle East, for whom Gesha is a luxury good, compete fiercely for competition lots.
  • Prestige. Gesha is to coffee today what the most famous estates are to wine.

That doesn't mean every Gesha costs thousands. It is now also grown in Colombia, Costa Rica, Ethiopia and Peru, and you can enjoy a good Gesha from these countries at a more reasonable price, though still at several times the price of regular specialty coffee.

Gesha or Geisha?

Both. Gesha is the more accurate spelling, after the Ethiopian area it comes from. Geisha arose as a corruption of the name and has nothing to do with Japanese geishas. In practice both are used, and you will see both on coffee bags.

Other coffee varieties worth tasting

SL28 and SL34

Kenyan varieties named after Scott Agricultural Laboratories, which selected and distributed them in the 1930s. Kenyan coffee owes its fame to them: juicy, vivid acidity and notes of blackcurrant, grapefruit and tomato. They are prone to disease, which is why Kenya also grows the more resistant Ruiru 11 and Batian.

Caturra and Catuaí

Caturra is a natural mutation of Bourbon discovered in Brazil. It has short, compact trees, so it can be planted more densely and yields more. Catuaí was bred in Brazil by crossing Mundo Novo and Caturra. Both are among the most widespread varieties in Latin America and taste sweet and balanced; they are the backbone of many coffees from Colombia, Costa Rica and Honduras.

Pacamara

A Salvadoran cross between Pacas and Maragogype, with giant beans. Pacamara has a bold, sometimes even wild flavor with notes of tropical fruit, herbs and chocolate, and it is a favorite in quality competitions.

Maragogype

A mutation of Typica discovered in Brazil, known as the "elephant bean" because of its unusually large beans. It tastes delicate and clean but has a low yield.

Ethiopian native varieties

Ethiopia is home to thousands of local varieties that developed naturally over centuries. On bags they are often grouped together as "heirloom" or "landrace", and sometimes you will see numbers such as 74110 or 74112: these are varieties selected by the Ethiopian research center in Jimma. This genetic diversity is one of the reasons Ethiopian coffees are so varied and aromatic.

Castillo and other resistant varieties

Because of coffee leaf rust, researchers have bred varieties that resist the disease. The best known is Colombia's Castillo, which now makes up a large share of Colombian production. Resistant varieties long had a reputation for inferior flavor, but with good care and processing they can also produce excellent specialty coffee.

Sidra and Pink Bourbon: the new stars

Ecuador's Sidra and Colombia's Pink Bourbon are varieties that have been talked about a lot in recent years. Both offer pronounced floral and fruity flavors and are showing up in competitions and on roasters' menus. Interestingly, genetic analyses suggest they have less in common with the varieties they are named after than was long claimed, and are more closely related to Ethiopian varieties.

The most expensive coffee in the world: Gesha or kopi luwak?

Search for "the most expensive coffee in the world" and you will often come across kopi luwak, an Indonesian coffee whose beans have passed through the digestive tract of a civet. It sells for high prices thanks to its unusual story. In terms of quality, though, it is not specialty coffee: the flavor tends to be flat, and above all its production raises a number of ethical problems, because the civets are often kept in cages and force-fed coffee cherries.

The truly most expensive coffee in the world is Panamanian Gesha. Its price reflects flavor, rarity and the farmers' meticulous work, not an exotic story. If you want to taste something exceptional, reach for Gesha, not kopi luwak.

How to find the variety on the bag

Most specialty roasters list the variety on the bag or in the coffee's description, usually right next to the country of origin and the processing method. Sometimes it is a single variety, sometimes several (for example "Caturra, Castillo") because the farmer grows more than one variety and harvests them together. On Ethiopian coffees you will find "heirloom" or specific variety numbers.

If the variety is missing from the bag, that doesn't mean the coffee is bad, but you lose part of its story. We explain how to read a specialty coffee bag in How to Choose Coffee Beans.

How to recognize varieties in practice

The best way to find your way around varieties is to taste. When you drink coffees from different countries and varieties side by side, you will soon start to recognize the juiciness of a Kenyan SL28, the sweetness of a Rwandan Bourbon or the floral character of a Gesha. Frequently asked questions

Is Gesha really the best coffee in the world?

Gesha is among the most distinctive and highly rated coffees and achieves top scores in competitions. Whether it will be the best for you depends on your taste: if you like full-bodied, chocolatey coffees, its tea-like delicacy may disappoint you.

What is the difference between a coffee variety and a coffee species?

A species is a botanical category, such as arabica or robusta. A variety is a variant within a species, such as Bourbon, Typica or Gesha within arabica.

What does "heirloom" mean on Ethiopian coffee?

It is a label for a mix of local, native Ethiopian varieties that grow in a given area and are usually not identified individually.

How much does Gesha cost?

It depends on origin and quality. Gesha from Colombia or Ethiopia usually costs two to several times as much as regular specialty coffee, while competition Panamanian Geshas are sold in small packs at luxury-goods prices.

Why is coffee grown in so many varieties?

Because each variety offers a different trade-off between flavor, yield and resistance. Farmers choose based on the conditions on their farm, demand and the risk of disease.
