Want to learn how to taste coffee well enough to really notice the difference between an Ethiopian and a Brazilian, or between a washed and a natural coffee? Professionals use a simple, precise method for this: cupping. Roasters use it to select green coffee, baristas use it to build their menus, and judges use it at competitions. The good news is that you don't need any expensive equipment to cup at home. A few coffees, hot water, a spoon and half an hour are enough. This guide takes you through it step by step and shows you how to describe what you taste.

Quick answer: Grind 2–4 different coffees (about 11–12 g of each), put them in separate cups and pour 200 ml of water at around 93 °C over each. After four minutes, break the crust on the surface with a spoon, skim off the foam and let the coffee cool. Then taste by slurping quickly from the spoon, comparing the coffees with each other as they gradually cool.

What cupping is and why professionals do it

Cupping is a standardized way of tasting coffee. The coffee isn't brewed through a filter or under pressure; it simply steeps in hot water. Every sample is prepared in exactly the same way: the same dose, the same grind size, the same water and the same timing. That way, the only difference between the cups is the coffee itself.

That is exactly what makes cupping so useful. In everyday brewing, a hundred factors affect how your coffee tastes, and you can never be sure whether a difference comes from the coffee or from how you made it. Cupping lets you compare coffees "naked." Professionals use it to score quality on the Specialty Coffee Association's 100-point scale, and a coffee that scores at least 80 points can be called specialty coffee. We explain more in our article Specialty Coffee vs. Regular Coffee.

At home, though, you don't need to worry about points. Cupping helps you figure out what you like, learn to put flavors into words and choose coffee more confidently.

What you need for cupping at home

  • 2–4 different coffees. You'll learn the most if they are as different as possible, for example a washed Ethiopian, a natural Brazilian and a Colombian.
  • A burr grinder, ideally one with adjustable grind size.
  • A scale accurate to at least one gram, ideally to a tenth of a gram.
  • Identical cups or bowls holding 200–250 ml, one per coffee (or two, if you want a control sample).
  • A kettle and, ideally, a thermometer. Use filtered or good bottled water; hard tap water dulls the flavor of coffee.
  • Two tablespoons for skimming the foam and one spoon for tasting. Professionals use deep cupping spoons, which you can buy for around €10–30.
  • A glass of hot water for rinsing the spoon between samples.
  • Paper and a pencil for notes.
  • A timer – your phone will do.

Home cupping step by step

1. Prepare the coffee and water

The Specialty Coffee Association's professional standard uses a ratio of 8.25 g of coffee to 150 ml of water. For home cups, we'll simplify that to roughly 11–12 g of coffee per 200 ml of water. What matters most is that the ratio is the same for every sample. Label the cups or put slips of paper with the coffee names underneath them; after a while you would mix them up.

Heat the water to about 93 °C. If you don't have a thermometer, let the water stand for about 30 seconds after it boils.

2. Grind the coffee

Grind each coffee separately, right before cupping, at a medium-coarse setting – about the same as for a French press, or slightly finer. Between coffees, run a few beans of the next coffee through the grinder and throw them away so the samples don't mix. Put the ground coffee into the cups.

3. Smell the dry coffee

Before you pour any water, smell each cup. This dry smell is called the fragrance. Try to write down your first impression: is the smell more sweet, fruity, floral, nutty or spicy? There are no wrong answers.

4. Pour the water

Start the timer and fill each cup with water, one after another, right up to the rim. Pour so that all of the coffee gets wet. A crust of coffee grounds will start to form on the surface. Don't stir now; let the coffee steep for four minutes.

5. Break the crust

After four minutes comes the most aromatic moment of the whole cupping. Put your nose right above the cup and gently push the crust away from you with the spoon three times. An intense smell is released, called the aroma. Note how it differs from the dry fragrance. Rinse the spoon between cups.

6. Skim off the foam

Once the crust is broken, most of the grounds sink to the bottom. Some foam and small particles stay on the surface, though. Carefully scoop them off with two spoons and throw them away so they don't get in the way when you taste.

7. Let the coffee cool and taste

About 10–12 minutes after pouring, when the coffee has cooled to around 70 °C, start tasting. Take a little coffee from the surface with the spoon and slurp it quickly and loudly. Slurping isn't bad manners; it's technique. The coffee sprays across your whole mouth and mixes with air, so the aromas rise into your nose. That is where we perceive most of what we call flavor.

Taste each coffee in turn, rinsing the spoon in between and sipping some water if you need to.

8. Keep tasting as the coffee cools

The most important tip comes last: don't taste just once. Coffee changes as it cools. When it's hot, you mostly notice intensity and bitterness; as it cools, sweetness, acidity and fruity notes come forward. Go through all the samples three or four times over twenty to thirty minutes. It's at lukewarm temperatures that you can best tell how clean and high-quality a coffee is.

How to describe what you taste

At first you may feel that all the coffees taste "like coffee." That's normal. It helps to focus on one attribute at a time:

Acidity

Not in the sense of "gone off," but as the freshness you know from fruit. Is the acidity gentle, like an apple, sharper, like citrus, or juicy, like blackcurrant? We explain why acidity in specialty coffee isn't a flaw in our article Why "Sour" Coffee Doesn't Have to Scare You.

Sweetness

Good coffee is naturally sweet, even without sugar. Does the sweetness remind you more of caramel, honey, cane sugar or ripe fruit?

Body

How does the coffee feel in your mouth? Light and tea-like, smooth like milk, or thick and velvety like cream?

Aftertaste

What stays in your mouth after you swallow? Is the aftertaste long and pleasant, or short, dry or bitter?

Flavor notes

Here it helps to move from the general to the specific. First ask yourself whether the coffee is fruity or more nutty and chocolatey. If it's fruity, is the fruit citrus, stone fruit, berries or tropical? If it's berries, is it closer to strawberry, blueberry or blackcurrant? That's exactly how the coffee flavor wheel used by professionals works – and you can find it online and print it out.

The flavor notes on coffee bags work the same way. When a roaster writes blueberry, jasmine, milk chocolate, it doesn't mean the coffee is flavored; it simply describes what they tasted in it.

Balance

How well do all the attributes fit together? Does the acidity overpower the sweetness? Is the coffee harmonious, or does something "stick out"?

How to take notes

You don't need a professional cupping form. A simple table on paper is enough: for each coffee, rate the acidity, sweetness, body and aftertaste on a scale of one to five, for example, and add two or three words that come to mind. At the end, write down which coffee you liked best, and why.

After a few sessions like this, you'll start to see patterns. Maybe you'll find that you enjoy natural coffees from Africa the most, or that you always reach for a sweet Colombian. That's exactly the kind of information that will help you choose coffee next time.

The most common home cupping mistakes

  • Different doses of coffee. If one cup has more coffee in it, it will be stronger and the comparison loses its point. Weigh it.
  • Tasting coffee that's too hot. You'll taste almost nothing in scalding coffee. Wait.
  • Tasting only once. Coffee changes as it cools, so taste it repeatedly.
  • Too many samples at once. Two to four coffees are plenty to start with. More will overwhelm you.
  • Strong smells nearby. Perfume, scented candles or cooking in the kitchen will spoil your cupping.
  • Hard tap water. Its minerals mute both acidity and aromas.
  • Fear of a "silly" answer. If a coffee reminds you of your grandmother's blackcurrant cake, write that down. Your associations are just as valid as the ones on the bag.

Frequently asked questions

Why do you slurp when cupping?

A quick slurp sprays the coffee across your whole mouth and aerates it. The aromatic compounds then reach your nose, where we perceive most flavor nuances. Without slurping, you would mostly taste the basic tastes: acidity, sweetness and bitterness.

Do I need cupping bowls and spoons?

No. Any identical cups holding 200–250 ml and ordinary tablespoons will do. Cupping spoons are deeper and more comfortable, but you don't need them to get started.

What's the difference between taste and aroma?

Your tongue perceives only five basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Everything else – strawberries, jasmine, chocolate – you perceive through your nose, either directly when you sniff or from the back of your mouth when you swallow. That's why slurping matters so much when tasting.

Why taste several coffees at once?

Because human taste is relative. A single coffee on its own is hard to describe, but when you compare it with another, the differences stand out immediately. Suddenly one coffee seems fruitier, another sweeter and a third fuller.

Can I cup espresso coffee too?

Yes. Professionals also use cupping to evaluate coffees they later brew as espresso. Just bear in mind that darker roasted coffees will come across as more bitter and intense on the cupping table.
