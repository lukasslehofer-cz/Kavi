If they already have plenty of coffee, go for equipment that improves their brewing: a hand grinder, a digital scale or a pour-over dripper. And if you're not sure at all, give a voucher.

Before you start: how do they drink their coffee?

The most common mistake with coffee gifts is that they don't match the way the person actually makes coffee. A bag of ground coffee will please someone with a moka pot, but it will more likely disappoint an enthusiast with a hand grinder. Before you go shopping, try to quietly find out the answers to four questions:

  • What do they brew with? A bean-to-cup machine, an espresso machine, a moka pot, a French press, or a manual brewer such as a V60 or AeroPress?
  • Do they drink it black or with milk? That decides whether to choose coffee roasted for espresso or for filter.
  • Do they grind their own beans? If they don't have a grinder at home, that's a gift that will make them happy and improve the taste of their coffee more than anything else.
  • Are they a beginner or an experienced coffee drinker? Beginners mostly enjoy discovering new flavors; more advanced drinkers tend to appreciate precise equipment or a rare coffee.

One or two answers are enough to narrow things down a lot. And if you can't find out anything, that's fine – for each idea below, we say who it suits.

4. A hand coffee grinder

If they still buy pre-ground coffee, give them a grinder. It will do more for the taste of their coffee than a new coffee maker. Ground coffee loses its aroma within minutes to hours, while whole beans keep it for weeks – we explain this in detail in Whole Bean vs Ground Coffee.

Choose a burr grinder, not a blade “chopper” that smashes the beans unevenly into a mix of dust and coarse chunks. A good hand grinder with steel burrs costs from about €80; an electric grinder with comparable grind quality tends to be more expensive.

Who it's for: anyone who brews with a French press, a pour-over dripper, an AeroPress or a moka pot. Owners of a bean-to-cup machine don't need one – it has a grinder built in.

5. A digital scale with a timer

An unassuming but extremely useful gift. Good coffee depends on the ratio of coffee to water, and neither a scoop nor a spoon is accurate – beans from different coffees have different densities. A coffee scale accurate to 0.1 g with a built-in timer helps them brew equally good coffee day after day. Basic models are affordable (around €10–30); those with an auto-start timer or flow-rate measurement cost more.

Who it's for: owners of pour-over drippers, AeroPresses and espresso machines.

6. A V60, Chemex or AeroPress

For someone who so far drinks only espresso or coffee from a machine, manual brewing can be a small revelation. Filter coffee brings out delicate fruity and floral notes that easily get lost in espresso. Three brewers are the most popular:

  • Hario V60 – a classic cone dripper that's great for experimenting. One of the cheapest ways to get started.
  • AeroPress – durable, fast and ideal for travel. It's forgiving, so beginners enjoy it too.
  • Chemex – an elegant glass carafe with thick paper filters that makes clean coffee for several people at once.

Don't forget to add paper filters and ideally some filter coffee as well. You'll find brewing instructions in our article How to Make Coffee at Home: 4 Methods.

7. A gooseneck kettle

The narrow, curved spout lets you pour water slowly and precisely, which is key for pour-over. Electric versions with adjustable temperature are a small luxury an enthusiast will appreciate every morning – light roasts are brewed at roughly 92–96 °C, and the difference compared with boiling water shows in the cup.

Who it's for: people who already have a dripper and want to take their brewing further.

8. A book about coffee

A book is a gift a coffee lover keeps coming back to. Two that are worth it:

  • Jessica Easto: How to Taste Coffee – uses practical exercises to teach you to recognize the flavors and aromas in your cup. Great for anyone who wants to understand more about what they're drinking.
  • James Hoffmann: The World Atlas of Coffee – a comprehensive, beautifully illustrated guide to growing countries, varieties and coffee processing.

9. A brewing or latte art course

An experience instead of a thing. Many roasters and cafés run courses in manual brewing, espresso or latte art, and in a few hours the recipient will learn more than in months of trial and error at home. Choose courses with smaller groups, where there's enough time for hands-on practice.

Who it's for: new owners of an espresso machine and anyone who likes to learn.

10. A coffee tasting at home

Coffee drinkers who already know what they like want to be able to name the flavors too. Put together a small tasting kit for them: two to four different coffees, two spoons and instructions. You can taste coffee without professional equipment – we explain how in How to Taste Coffee at Home. Three coffees from different countries in one coffee box make an ideal base for a side-by-side tasting like this.

11. A quality cup, mug or travel mug

A classic that works, as long as you choose quality. Thick-walled porcelain cups that hold heat suit espresso; thin-walled mugs, in which aromas come through better, suit filter coffee. Anyone who drinks coffee on the go will appreciate a travel mug that seals reliably and is easy to clean.

12. A coffee outing

Take them to a roastery, a coffee festival or a café they've long wanted to visit. Coffee festivals now take place in many European cities and are a great chance to taste coffees from dozens of roasters in one place. If you're planning a trip abroad, you'll find inspiration in our guide to the best coffee roasters in Europe.

What to avoid when choosing a coffee gift

  • Ground coffee without a roast date. You don't know how old it is, and a real coffee lover probably won't use it anyway.
  • Flavored coffees with vanilla or hazelnut. The flavoring often masks poor-quality beans, and coffee lovers steer well clear of them.
  • A coffee machine without asking first. It's a big investment and a very personal choice – it's better to contribute to a model they pick themselves.
  • Capsules for a different system than the one they have at home. Check beforehand.
  • A blade grinder. It grinds unevenly and is more likely to harm the coffee than improve it.
  • Big “gift hampers” with coffee of unknown origin. One bag of genuinely good coffee beats five average ones.

Frequently asked questions

What gift should I give someone who uses a bean-to-cup machine?

Whole-bean coffee roasted for espresso. Bean-to-cup machines grind the beans themselves and cope best with a darker, espresso roast. With a KAVI gift subscription, the recipient chooses Espresso as the coffee type when activating it.

Is it better to give whole-bean or ground coffee?

Whole beans, if the recipient has a grinder or a bean-to-cup machine. Whole beans keep their aroma much longer. Only give ground coffee to someone who doesn't have a grinder and doesn't want one – and in that case, consider including a grinder with the gift.
