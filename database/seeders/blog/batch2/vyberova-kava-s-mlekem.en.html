Does specialty coffee with milk make sense, or is it a waste to "dilute" it? There is a notion among coffee enthusiasts that specialty coffee should only be drunk black, ideally as pour-over. The reality is different: most of the coffee that specialty cafés sell is cappuccino, flat white or latte. Good coffee doesn't disappear in milk. On the contrary, with the right coffee you get a drink that is sweeter, creamier and richer in flavor than anything made with ordinary coffee. In this article we explain which specialty coffee to choose for milk, how the various milk drinks differ and how to make them at home.

Quick answer: The best specialty coffee for milk is roasted for espresso, with notes of chocolate, caramel and nuts, typically from Brazil, Colombia, Guatemala or Honduras. A natural Ethiopian also works brilliantly; with milk it tastes like a strawberry milkshake. Very light, acidic filter coffees are less suited to milk. Steam milk to 55–65 °C, never above 70 °C.

The myth: specialty coffee is only for drinking black

This idea came about for a good reason: subtle flavors shine best in filter coffee, and specialty roasters like to promote it. But that doesn't mean specialty coffee is wasted in milk. You will taste the difference immediately between a cappuccino made with fresh specialty coffee and one made with coffee that has sat on a shelf for months: the first is sweet, clean and full of flavor, the second bitter and flat.

So specialty coffee with milk is a perfectly legitimate choice. You just need to know which coffee to pick.

What happens to coffee flavor in milk

Milk is not a neutral diluent. It changes the flavor of coffee in several ways:

  • It softens acidity and bitterness. The proteins and fat in milk round off sharp flavors, so the coffee comes across as gentler.
  • It adds sweetness. Lactose, the sugar in milk, is clearly noticeable at the right temperature, which is why well-steamed milk tastes sweet on its own.
  • It dilutes intensity. Delicate, tea-like coffees easily get "lost" in milk, while bold coffees with a full body cut through.
  • It highlights certain notes. Chocolate, caramel, nuts and sweet berries pair beautifully with milk. Citrus and floral notes tend to fade.

Which specialty coffee works with milk

Roast: for espresso

Coffees roasted for espresso have a fuller body, more developed sweetness and a bolder flavor that doesn't get lost in milk. Filter roasts are lighter, more delicate and more acidic, and in milk they can taste watery or sour. We explain the differences in Espresso Roast or Filter Roast?

Origin and flavor profile

The most reliable choices for milk are coffees with notes of milk chocolate, caramel, hazelnut and dried fruit:

  • Brazil: chocolate, nuts, low acidity, full body. A classic for cappuccino.
  • Colombia: caramel, red apple, sweetness. An all-rounder.
  • Guatemala: dark chocolate, spice, full body.
  • Honduras and Peru: sweet and mellow, with notes of caramel and nuts.

To find out what coffees from individual countries taste like, read Which Country Has the Best Coffee?

Fruity coffees with milk: when it works

Not all fruity coffees are off-limits with milk. Natural Ethiopian coffees with notes of strawberry and blueberry make a drink that many people compare to a strawberry milkshake or blueberry yogurt. It works because berry sweetness complements milk. If you want to try something unusual, it is a great experiment.

What to avoid

Very lightly roasted washed coffees with pronounced citrus acidity are less suited to milk; typical examples are some Kenyan or washed Ethiopian filter coffees. In milk they can taste sour and bland. Enjoy these coffees black.

Cappuccino, flat white, latte and cortado: what's the difference

Milk drinks differ mainly in the ratio of coffee to milk and in the amount of foam. The volumes given are approximate; every café does it a little differently:

  • Cortado: espresso with roughly the same to twice the amount of warm milk, with almost no foam. A small drink (around 100–120 ml) with a pronounced coffee flavor.
  • Cappuccino: espresso and steamed milk with a layer of foam about 1–1.5 cm thick. Around 150–180 ml in total. A balanced ratio of coffee to milk.
  • Flat white: a double espresso (often a ristretto) and milk with a thin layer of fine microfoam. Similar in volume to a cappuccino, but with a stronger coffee flavor.
  • Caffè latte: espresso and a larger amount of milk (250 ml or more) with a thin layer of foam. The mildest and milkiest drink.
  • Espresso macchiato: espresso "stained" with a spoonful of milk foam.

Tip: The more delicate and fruity your coffee, the less milk you should use. Bold, chocolatey coffees can handle even a large latte, while a delicate natural Ethiopian shines more in a cortado or a small flat white.

How to make coffee with milk at home

Espresso machine with a steam wand

The best results at home. Make an espresso following our guide How to Make Espresso at Home and steam the milk with the steam wand (see the steps below).

Bean-to-cup machine

Most automatic machines make cappuccino and latte at the touch of a button. Choose an espresso roast with notes of chocolate and nuts, which behaves reliably in an automatic machine. Clean the milk system regularly: milk residue quickly ruins the taste of your drink.

Moka pot and milk frother

An inexpensive and surprisingly good combination. The moka pot makes a strong coffee base, and you froth the milk with a manual or electric frother. The result isn't espresso in the true sense, but it makes a tasty "homemade cappuccino".

AeroPress or French press

With an AeroPress you can make a concentrated coffee base (for example 18 g of coffee to 80–100 ml of water). A French press can double as a milk frother: fill it a third of the way with warm milk and froth it with quick strokes of the plunger.

How to steam milk

  1. Use cold milk and a cold jug. It gives you more time to steam.
  2. Fill the jug about a third of the way, as the volume grows as you steam.
  3. Place the wand tip just below the surface and turn on the steam. The milk should hiss gently; that is how air gets into it. Stretch the milk longer for a cappuccino and for a shorter time for a flat white or latte.
  4. Once the milk has increased in volume, lower the wand deeper and let the milk spin in a whirlpool. This breaks large bubbles down into fine microfoam.
  5. Stop at 55–65 °C. At that point the jug feels hot to the touch but you can still hold it. Above 70 °C milk loses its sweetness and takes on a cooked taste.
  6. Tap the jug on the counter and swirl it so the foam blends with the milk into a glossy, smooth texture. Then pour it into the espresso straight away.

Well-steamed milk looks like wet white paint: glossy and smooth, with no visible bubbles.

Which milk to choose

  • Whole cow's milk (3.5%): the best texture and sweetness. The ideal choice to start with.
  • Semi-skimmed milk: it foams, but the foam is thinner and less creamy.
  • Lactose-free milk: tastes sweeter because the lactose has been broken down into simpler sugars. It foams well.
  • Oat "barista" drinks: the most popular plant-based alternative. They foam much like cow's milk and have a neutral, slightly cereal-like taste.
  • Soy and almond drinks: usable, but they can curdle in more acidic coffee. Choose the barista versions.

Iced coffee with milk and cold brew

Specialty coffee with milk doesn't have to be hot. In summer, iced lattes and cold brew with milk are popular, and here too the quality of the coffee matters.

An iced latte is simple to make: put ice in a tall glass, pour in cold milk (about 150–200 ml) and top it with a freshly made double espresso. The coffee gradually mixes with the milk and creates a lovely gradient. The ice dilutes the coffee somewhat, so choose a bolder coffee with notes of chocolate or caramel, or make the espresso a little stronger.

Cold brew is coffee steeped in cold water in the fridge for 12 to 18 hours. For a concentrate that you then dilute with milk, use a ratio of roughly 1:8 (for example 100 g of coarsely ground coffee to 800 ml of water). Strain the finished concentrate through a filter and serve it over ice with milk at a ratio of about 1:1. Cold extraction gives a coffee with low acidity and pronounced sweetness that works wonderfully with milk; natural coffees from Brazil or Ethiopia are an excellent fit.

Don't steam milk for cold drinks with hot steam. If you want foam, use cold foam from a frother that can whip milk without heating it.

Most common mistakes

  • Overheated milk. Above 70 °C it loses its sweetness and the foam falls apart.
  • Stale coffee. Even in milk you can tell when coffee has lost its aroma: the drink is flat and bitter. Keep an eye on the roast date.
  • A filter roast that is too light. In milk it tastes sour and watery.
  • Too much milk for a delicate coffee. A large latte made with a delicate coffee tastes mostly of milk.
  • A badly adjusted grinder. Under-extracted espresso tastes sour and disappears in milk. Over-extracted espresso is bitter and astringent.
  • Adding sugar straight away. Try it without sugar first: good coffee with well-steamed milk is sweet on its own.

Frequently asked questions

Is it a waste to put specialty coffee in milk?

No. Specialty coffee with milk tastes much better than regular coffee with milk. Just choose a coffee that suits milk: an espresso roast with notes of chocolate, caramel or berries.

What is the best coffee for cappuccino?

A medium espresso roast from Brazil, Colombia or Guatemala. If you want to experiment, try a natural Ethiopian.

What is the difference between a flat white and a cappuccino?

A flat white is made with a double espresso and has a thinner layer of finer foam, so it tastes more strongly of coffee. A cappuccino has more foam and is milder.

Why does my cappuccino taste sour?

Most often because of under-extracted espresso (the coffee is ground too coarse or runs too fast), coffee that is too fresh, or coffee roasted for filter. Try a finer grind and an espresso roast. We explain why coffee tastes sour in Why "Sour" Coffee Doesn't Have to Scare You.

What is the ideal milk temperature?

55–65 °C. In this range milk is at its sweetest and the foam at its most stable. Above 70 °C both flavor and texture change.

Can I have specialty coffee with milk decaffeinated too?

Yes. Good decaf coffees taste great today, including with milk. Read more in Decaf Coffee: Myths and Facts.
